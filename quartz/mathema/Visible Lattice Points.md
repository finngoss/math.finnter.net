A lattice point is some $x\in\Z^n$. A visible lattice point is one such that $\gcd(x)=\gcd(x_1,...,x_n)=1$. 

The term 'visible' comes from imagining an observer positioned at the origin. For an example in $\Z^2$, the point $(3,4)$ is thought to be visible whereas $(6,8)$ is invisible, since the line of sight to that point is blocked by $(3,4)$. Thus an equivalent definition is a point such that the line segment connecting it to the origin intersects no other points. The set of visible lattice points is the set of unique directions in $\Z^n$. 

One may also define visibility from a point other than the origin, or even joint visibility, which requires visibility from a set of points.

If you consider the [[ideals]] of the ring of integers generated by the coordinates of a lattice point, the ideal generated by a visible point is the whole ring.

Visible lattice points are the first topic I did mathematics research on! I worked with Kelly McKinnie at the University of Montana on [this paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22544), in which we calculate the density of visible lattice points on hyperplanes and their intersections. Researching these objects was the first time I felt like I was actually *doing* real mathematics, rather than learning and regurgitating somebody else's (not that there's anything wrong with that!). I also fancy it for its accessibility to non mathematicians as well as the access it provided me as an undergraduate to the world of [[Analytic Number Theory]]. As I continued research, my appreciation for them grew as I truly see them as an object with ample grounds for connection between different fields of math.
